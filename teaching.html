UCL modules

Both modules are 15-credit, Level 7 (postgraduate) modules taught in person in Term 2, and are assessed half through in-class activity and half through coursework.

Algorithmic Trading

  • COMP0051
  • 15 credits · Level 7
  • Term 2
  • Module leader since 2018/19

An introduction to algorithmic trading and risk-premia strategies: why they work, what their statistical properties are, and how to design, implement and evaluate them. The module surveys the main families of strategies used in industry, points to open areas of research including recent machine-learning approaches, and gives students the tools to build strategies and to test their known and provable properties rigorously.

Topics

  • The trading industry, market structure and data sources
  • Families of trading and risk-premia strategies
  • Limit order book dynamics
  • Portfolio theory and portfolio construction
  • Statistical analysis and evaluation of strategies
  • Sharpe ratio and other performance metrics
  • Backtest overfitting, multiple hypothesis testing and model validation

Learning outcomes

  • Analyse trading strategies statistically
  • Research, design and develop new strategies

Background expected

Probability and statistics, mathematical analysis, and fluency in a scientific programming language (Python is used in class).

Advanced Machine Learning in Finance

  • COMP0162
  • 15 credits · Level 7
  • Term 2
  • Module leader since 2021/22

Modern machine-learning methods applied to the problems that matter in finance: derivatives pricing and model calibration, portfolio allocation and hedging, investment decisions, credit scoring, fraud detection and risk management. The module also introduces text mining as a source of alternative data. It is hands-on throughout: students implement models in Python with Keras/TensorFlow on real and simulated financial data, with an emphasis on noisy, non-stationary datasets.

Topics

  • Neural network fundamentals, layer types and architectures
  • Optimisation: loss functions, regularisation, stochastic gradient descent, overfitting and generalisation
  • Supervised deep learning: CNNs, RNNs and LSTMs
  • Unsupervised learning: PCA and autoencoders
  • Reinforcement learning for dynamic control problems in finance
  • Graph neural networks and learning on financial networks
  • Text mining and sentiment analysis; time-series prediction

Example projects

  • Network filtering and anomaly detection
  • Link prediction in temporal networks
  • Autoencoders for payment-network analysis
  • Return forecasting with recurrent networks
  • Sentiment analysis of financial text

Learning outcomes

  • Describe the main advanced algorithms (CNNs, RNNs, reinforcement learning) and choose the right one for a financial problem
  • Speed up pricing and calibration, and formulate dynamic control problems in finance
  • Build flexible models for financial prediction and analyse textual data algorithmically
  • Write and adapt machine-learning code in Python with Keras and TensorFlow

Background expected

Probability, linear algebra and multivariate calculus; programming in a numerical language; and Machine Learning with Applications in Finance (COMP0050 or COMP0198).

PhD teaching

Statistical Physics for Machine Learning

Phase transitions, inference and high-dimensional landscapes

  • PhD in Data Science · Sapienza University of Rome
  • DIAG
  • 20 hours · 5 days × 4 h
  • 25–29 May 2026

A doctoral course delivered at the Department of Computer, Control and Management Engineering (DIAG) of Sapienza, my alma mater. It treats learning as a problem in statistical mechanics: starting from entropy, free energy and Bayesian inference, maximum likelihood, regularisation and posterior inference become instances of the same physics, and phase transitions explain when learning becomes hard. Each day mixes lectures, whiteboard derivations, exercises and open problems.

Programme

  • Foundations — entropy, free energy and Bayesian inference; learning as statistical mechanics
  • Disordered systems — random-field Ising model, Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass, p-spin model, random k-SAT
  • Methods — replica, cavity and mean-field approaches
  • Random matrix theory — semicircle law, Stieltjes transform, BBP transition
  • Applications — PCA and community detection, Hopfield networks, loss-landscape Hessians and the edge of stability, diffusion models
  • Open problems — overparameterised networks, implicit regularisation, the limits of high-dimensional generation

Background expected

Linear algebra, probability and basic calculus. No prior knowledge of statistical physics or spin glasses is needed.

UCL IXN industry projects

The UCL Industry Exchange Network (IXN), running in Computer Science since 2011, embeds real industry problems into the degree. Each project pairs a UCL academic supervisor with a technical mentor from the partner organisation, so students work on a live problem while partners prototype ideas at low risk.

  • Master's students spend three months full-time on an IXN dissertation or team project over the summer (June–September). Confidential and closed-source projects are possible under NDA.
  • Undergraduates spend part of each year on industry projects during term, from team projects to final-year dissertations.
  • Partners range from large technology firms and international banks to insurers, start-ups, the NHS and charities.

I supervise IXN projects in quantitative finance, financial networks and machine learning. Organisations interested in proposing a project for next summer can get in touch; proposals for master's projects are usually collected between November and January.

Supervision

I supervise PhD and MSc projects on complex networks, random matrix theory and physics-informed machine learning, often with applications in finance. Prospective students are welcome to get in touch with a short description of their interests.
